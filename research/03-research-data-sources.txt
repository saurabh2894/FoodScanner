# Research: data sources, licences and regulatory pointers

Compiled 20 Nov 2026. Everything here is free and public.

## Open Food Facts (main test-data source)

- Crowdsourced, openly licensed database of packaged foods with a large India
  section (in.openfoodfacts.org). Free JSON API, no key needed for reads.
- Useful fields per product: `ingredients_text`, `nova_group`, `additives_tags`,
  `ingredients_analysis_tags`, plus photos of the ingredient panel on many
  records.
- The API already computes a NOVA group and additive list from an ingredient
  text. Treat this as a reference implementation to compare against, not as
  truth: the data is contributed by volunteers and Open Food Facts itself says
  it may be inaccurate or incomplete. A disagreement between your classifier
  and their record is something to inspect, not an automatic error on either
  side.
- Licence: Open Database License (ODbL) for the database, Database Contents
  License for the contents, CC-BY-SA for product images. You may use and adapt
  the data with attribution and share-alike. For a private portfolio project
  this is compatible; read the licences before any public release.
- Etiquette: send a descriptive User-Agent, cache responses, do not scrape at
  high volume.
- Docs: https://openfoodfacts.github.io/openfoodfacts-server/api/
  India data and licence: https://in.openfoodfacts.org/data

## FSSAI (regulatory context, India)

- Indian labelling is governed by the Food Safety and Standards (Labelling and
  Display) Regulations, 2020, with later amendments. They require an ingredient
  list in descending order of weight and additive declaration by functional
  class with INS numbers.
- Use FSSAI rules to know what a compliant label looks like, so the parser
  matches real packs. Do not turn this into a legal-compliance feature in the
  first version, and do not claim in the write-up that Indian rules let unsafe
  food through; that is an opinion, not a verified fact.
- Current regulations text: https://www.fssai.gov.in (search "Labelling and
  Display Regulations").

## What not to use

- Siga's score and category boundaries were proprietary. Its public method page
  (https://siga.care/indice-siga/) is inspiration for combining rule inputs;
  the score itself must not be copied or claimed.
- Nothing from any employer's internal material. Public sources and your own
  photos only.

## Collecting your own test set

Photograph 20 to 30 ingredient panels from products at home or a shop shelf.
Mix obvious cases (plain atta, a cola) with borderline ones (breads, breakfast
cereals, namkeens). Record your own NOVA call for each in labels.csv before
running the classifier.

Treat these labels as provisional reference labels, not scientific ground
truth. Before trusting the evaluation, do a second review pass over your own
labels on a different day, and check a sample against Open Food Facts' NOVA
data where a record exists. Where you remain unsure, mark the sample uncertain
and exclude it from accuracy, keeping it in the abstention analysis.

## Reading the evaluation

Report two separate numbers, never one:

- **Coverage**: how many samples the classifier answered versus abstained on.
- **Accuracy on answered samples**: how often answered samples matched the
  reference label.

A classifier that answers everything but is often wrong, and one that abstains
on everything, are both failures; the interesting result is high accuracy at
useful coverage, and the write-up should show both.
